Comparing twelve projective rays

A vector can change while its ray stays the same. Keep the integer data, compare two rays, and discover the first layer of projective states.

Keep the data. Remove the scale.

A ray is a nonzero vector up to a common nonzero factor. Here its coordinates belong to K = ℚ(j), with j = e2πi/5. Clearing denominators gives integer coefficients in 𝒪 = ℤ[j].

Keep this integral pair(R, s) = (vv†, v†v)
→
It represents this projectorρ = R / s

The dagger † means conjugate transpose. We can compare projectors by cross-multiplying the stored pairs: swRv = svRw. No normalizing square root is required.

For example, (1,1) and (2,2) give the same ray. Their pairs are (R,2) and (4R,8), where every entry of R is 1. The factor cancels exactly.

How small can a ray be?

First choose a primitive integral vector: its coordinates generate all of 𝒪. For this representative, take s = v†v and multiply its two real readings. The result is a positive integer height, Δ, independent of the primitive representative.

One nonzero coordinateΔ ≥ 1Two axis rays reach 1
Two nonzero coordinatesΔ ≥ 4Ten phase rays reach 4

The general bound is Δ ≥ k² for k nonzero coordinates. In two coordinates, exactly twelve rays have Δ ≤ 4: the two axes and [1 : εr], for r = 0,…,9, where ε = −j³ = eπi/5. No rays have height 2 or 3.

Height refers to the chosen coordinate basis. This is the lowest layer, not the entire projective space.

A complete example, readable without JavaScript

Two fifth-root phases

Take v = (1,1) and w = (1,j). Their inner product is 1 + j, and both squared lengths are 2. Because (1 + j)(1 + j⁴) = 1 + φ,

P = (1 + φ) / 4 = (3 + √5) / 8

The second reading is (3 − √5)/8. Both are strictly between 0 and 1. In the selector these are phase 0 and phase 2, since ε² = j.

Paper: this exact example ↗

One number. Two valid readings.

The exact overlap lies in the real field ℚ(φ). Replacing φ by either root of x² − x − 1 = 0 gives its two real embeddings. Each is an ordinary complex-projector overlap after the corresponding embedding of K, so each lies between 0 and 1.

There is one algebraic result, not a choice between two numerical estimates of the same real value. The guide draws the phase circle in the first embedding and displays both overlap readings separately.

Try this: must different rays be orthogonal?

No. The default example gives (1 + φ)/4, neither 0 nor 1. Choose phase 5 against phase 0 for an orthogonal pair: (1,1) and (1,−1). Choose the same phase twice for overlap 1. An axis and any phase ray have overlap 1/2.

A precise language for a chosen class.

Every ray explored here belongs to ℙ¹(K). The full set of K-rational rays is countable and contains infinitely many more rays than these twelve. It does not include every ray over ℂ.

A common scalar, including J, changes a vector's integral data but leaves its projector unchanged. Producing a different projective ray requires a transformation that acts beyond common scaling.

For example, the integral matrix A = ((1,1),(1,−1)) maps [1:0] to [1:1] and satisfies A†A = 2I. It is invertible over K, but its determinant −2 is not a unit of 𝒪. This permits exact mixing without a normalizing √2.

Projectors and overlaps are standard mathematics. The paper specializes them to integral Twist coordinates and proves the sharp height bound and its lowest layer. These identities do not by themselves derive a physical sampling process or observed frequencies.
